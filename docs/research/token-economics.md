# SISY: the questions a token design has to answer

A note for [#21](https://github.com/sisyphus-network/Sisyphus/issues/21). It does not propose numbers. It sets out what has to be decided, what the chains and networks looked at for [#20](chain-design.md) and [#19](verification-prior-art.md) chose, and what follows from choices already made elsewhere in the design. Nothing here is advice on law or money, and the issue's last item stands: a lawyer before anything is issued or sold.

## What the token is for

Three uses come out of the other notes, and a design should be able to say which it serves:

1. **Paying for work.** A requester pays providers for compute and, later, storage.
2. **Something to lose.** Collateral for the nodes that coordinate and attest, and stakes for workers whose results are checked ([verification note](verification-next.md)). Without it, checking makes cheating likelier to be caught and never costly.
3. **Paying for the chain's security**, through block rewards.

A fourth use, speculation, arrives uninvited and is what the legal review is about.

## Supply and emission

What others do, each from its own documents (gathered 10 October 2026; see the chain note for links):

| Chain | Supply | Emission |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dash | About 17.7 to 18.9 million, depending on how much of the budget is spent | Subsidy falls by a fourteenth about every 383 days |
| Ravencoin | 21 thousand million | 5,000 a block, halving about every four years |
| Evrmore | *Unconfirmed* | 2,778 a block, halving about every 3.1 years; began with an airdrop to Ravencoin holders |

Choices to make:

- **A cap, or steady issue.** A cap is simple to state and makes the long-run security budget depend on fees. Steady issue pays for security for ever and dilutes holders for ever.
- **A smooth fall or halvings.** Dash's small yearly step avoids the shock a halving gives miners.
- **Whether unspent budget is created.** In Dash it is not, which makes the treasury a ceiling and not a pile.

## Who the block reward pays

Dash today: 20 per cent to miners, 60 to masternodes, 20 to the governance budget. Evrmore: 90 to miners, 10 to a development fund at fixed addresses.

Sisyphus adds a fourth party, compute providers. Two ways to pay them, and they are different economies:

- **From the requester.** Work is paid for by whoever wanted it. The block reward pays only for the chain. Akash, Golem and iExec work this way. Demand has to exist before providers earn.
- **From new coins.** Providers are paid from the block reward for work done, whoever asked. Bittensor and Gridcoin work this way, and Render burns what requesters pay while paying providers from a schedule of new coins. It pays providers before there is demand, which is why networks choose it, and it invites work done only to collect the reward. Every such scheme then needs a way to tell real work from manufactured work, which is the verification problem again with money on it.

A sober default: requesters pay providers; the block reward pays miners, masternodes and a budget; and any subsidy to providers from new coins is small, temporary and stated in advance.

## Founders, contributors and launch

The README promises transparent rules defined in advance. The options, plainly:

- **No allocation**, as Ravencoin had: everyone mines from the first block. Evrmore began with an airdrop to holders of another coin and a tenth of each block to a development fund.
- **An allocation with vesting**, stated before launch, with the addresses published.
- **A treasury voted by masternodes**, as in Dash, which funds development without anyone holding a founder's share, and puts the vote in the hands of whoever holds collateral.

Which of these is lawful to do, and where, is the lawyer's question and comes first.

## How a job's price is set

The three in the issue, with who uses them:

- **Posted by providers.** A provider states a price for a unit of work; the requester picks. Golem's market works this way.
- **Auctioned.** The requester posts a job, providers bid, the requester accepts one. Akash.
- **Fixed by the network.** Render prices jobs in ordinary money and burns the token equivalent.

A pool today has no prices, so the first version can be the simplest that works: the coordinator's owner sets a price for a unit of each kind of work in its pool. An open market between pools is a later problem and should not be designed before there is one pool that charges.

What a "unit" is has to be settled first: a task, a second of a core, a second of a graphics card, a token generated by a model. The daemon already reports tasks, their duration and the hardware of the worker that ran each, which is the raw material.

## Collateral and slashing

From the verification note: a stake only works if it can be taken faster than it can be withdrawn, and if taking it needs proof and not an accusation. Decisions:

- who holds stakes: workers, coordinators, both;
- how large, against what a cheat could gain from one job;
- what proves a fault: being outvoted is evidence and not proof, since honest work that is not deterministic is also outvoted;
- where a taken stake goes: burned, to whoever proved the fault, or to the requester who was wronged;
- how long a stake is locked after the work, so that a challenge has time to arrive.

Livepeer turned slashing off when its checks became matters of likelihood. That is the warning: slashing on a probabilistic check punishes the honest.

## Governance

Dash's process is the one to hand: proposals on chain, a vote by masternodes, a net tenth in favour to pass, paid monthly from a capped budget. It governs spending. It does not by itself govern rule changes, which in every chain of this family still happen by releasing software that enough nodes choose to run.

To decide: what can be changed by vote (the budget, prices, parameters), what needs a new release, and what is promised never to change.

## What can be done before a lawyer or a chain

- Decide the unit of work and have the daemon account for it in every job's record. This is useful to a private pool with no token: it is how an owner sees who did what.
- Write the candidate rules as a short document with every number left blank, and see whether two people read it the same way.
- Model one pool: so many jobs a day, so many providers, a price, a stake, a rate of checking. A spreadsheet answers whether honest providers earn more than the electricity and whether cheating pays.
